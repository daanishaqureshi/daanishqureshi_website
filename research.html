Physics-informed neural networks for drug release

Controlled-release films for patches, bandages and implants are usually characterised by running long release experiments. We built physics-informed and Bayesian neural networks that build Fick's diffusion law into training, so a short stretch of measurements predicts the long-term release. Benchmarked against the classical Fick, Higuchi and Peppas models on planar, wrinkled and crumpled films, the PINN reached an RMSE under 0.05 from the first 4% of the release data for planar films and from 25% for wrinkled and crumpled ones. Built in PyTorch and JAX. The paper joins the two Brown labs on this page: the modeling with Khemraj Shukla in Applied Mathematics, the release films from Vikas Srivastava's lab in Engineering.

D. A. Qureshi, K. Shukla, V. Srivastava. Drug release modeling using Physics-Informed Neural Networks. Journal of Drug Delivery Science and Technology 125, 108654 (2026).

Measuring embedded cracks in polyethylene with ultrasound

High-density polyethylene is used in nuclear plant cooling pipelines, gas pipelines and biomedical implants, and cracks hidden inside it can grow until the part fails. We trained a convolutional neural network entirely on finite element simulations of ultrasound, then applied it to real microsecond ultrasound signals. With no experimental data in training, it predicted crack length with a mean absolute percent error of 3.2% and crack position with 3.8%.

D. Aleem Qureshi, V. Bellala, S. Niu, V. Srivastava. A Machine Learning Method to Measure the Embedded Crack Length and Position in High-Density Polyethylene Using Ultrasound Time Signal. ASME Journal of Nondestructive Evaluation, Diagnostics and Prognostics of Engineering Systems 9(1), 011004 (2026).

Reading diet and health from photos of hands

Independent study in the lab. Unpublished

Can a photo of someone's hand say something about their diet and health? The pipeline calibrates color across cameras and lighting, finds and separates each hand, splits it into fingers and palm from keypoints, and extracts color and shape features on a common scale. The next step, proposed but not yet done, is to predict health outcomes from those features. If you are interested in this work, please reach out.

Why mussel byssal plaques are tiny yet strong

MIT, Department of Civil and Environmental Engineering, visiting student with Zhao Qin, 2018 to 2021

Mussels hold onto wave-battered rock with small adhesive plaques at the ends of their threads. Combining mechanical testing, 3D printing and numerical modeling, we showed why a small plaque, sized to its thread, already gives near-optimal attachment, and that the overall strength of the anchor depends more on the architecture of the thread network than on the size of each plaque.

D. A. Qureshi, S. Goffredo, Y. Kim, Y. Han, M. Guo, S. Ryu, Z. Qin. Why mussel byssal plaques are tiny yet strong in attachment. Matter 5(2), 710–724 (2022).
